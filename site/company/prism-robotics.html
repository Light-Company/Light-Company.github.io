A little light. A much clearer experiment.

Before a robot does anything, someone prepares its world. A cup is set down. A cable is moved. A drawer is left open just far enough. These small decisions become the starting point of the experiment, even when nobody writes them down.

Prism makes that starting point visible. A projector shows where objects belong. Cameras help check the scene against the intended setup. The record gives a trial a physical context: what was on the table, what was meant to happen and what should be examined again.

It is a system for the workcell around the robot. The robot keeps its own controller and policy; the operator keeps a meaningful role in preparing and reviewing the experiment.

A result should carry the room that made it.

See. Verify. Record. Return.

See
Bring the camera's view and the physical workspace into a shared frame. Registration connects a location in the image to a location the projector can mark.
Stage
Place the instruction on the table itself. Projected outlines and labels guide the operator toward the scene the experiment calls for.
Verify
Compare the observed scene with the target. A missing object or a placement mismatch should become a question before it becomes an unexplained result.
Record
Keep the setup with the episode. The technical workflow calls for object identities, positions and trial context to remain connected.
Return
Use a saved condition to guide another physical setup. Recreating a scene still requires an operator, calibration and verification; a digital record cannot move a cup by itself.

Projection is guidance during staging. If it is visible to the policy during recording, it may change the experiment. The trial protocol must account for that, including when guidance turns off.

Git for your physical space.

Software has a memory. You can name a version, understand what changed and return to an earlier state. A workbench usually has a photograph, a spreadsheet and the person who remembers where everything went.

“Git for your physical space” is our product direction: give a scene an identity; preserve its history; change one condition deliberately; compare what happened; make the important scene possible to find again.

That does not mean the world becomes perfectly deterministic. Objects wear. Cameras move. A soft cloth refuses to be the same cloth twice. A useful history preserves those differences rather than hiding them behind a clean success rate.

We have made that direction tangible in a scene-history concept. It shows the experience we want to build, with example revisions and a visible scene comparison. It is a product concept, separate from the current station software.

One number cannot describe a robot.

Start with a scene the policy can handle. Move the object. Rotate it. Introduce a distractor. Substitute an object. Change the light. One variable at a time gives the experiment a question you can actually answer.

The aim is a capability map: a picture of where the policy works, where it becomes uncertain and where it breaks under the conditions you tested. The gaps matter too. An untested condition should stay untested, rather than borrowing confidence from a neighboring cell.

Prism's projected staging directly helps with placement on a calibrated surface. Object choice, lighting, robot state and other conditions still need their own controls and records. See the capability-map guide for a practical way to structure the experiment.

Built around the real workcell.

A Prism setup brings together a projector, a camera, local compute, a calibrated workspace and the operator preparing the scene. The exact mounting, field of view and camera-to-projector relationship matter. Good software cannot repair a loose mount by describing it more confidently.

The process begins with the actual task: the objects, the reachable surface, the robot's view and what the team needs to compare. We discuss those conditions before proposing a station or an integration.

The platform story grows from that physical foundation. Records are useful when their calibration and task context remain attached. Comparisons are useful when the policies faced comparable conditions. A replay is useful when the recreated scene has been checked again.

Ambition, with the evidence beside it.

The lab films show projected guidance and real workcells. Our technical brief describes initial-condition control, camera checks, episode logging and re-projection, along with the limits that still need to be measured.

These are different kinds of evidence. A film demonstrates an interaction; it does not establish a placement tolerance or a universal improvement in policy performance. The brief's operational estimates come from a limited partner context, not a cross-industry study.

The public benchmark workspace currently uses illustrative data to show the intended analysis experience. It is not a measured ranking of model providers. Scene branching, collaborative history and automated regression scheduling on this site are proposed direction unless explicitly identified otherwise.

Questions worth asking.

Is Prism a simulator?

Prism Robotics is centered on physical workcells. Simulation can help explore possible conditions; a physical evaluation still needs its own setup, observation and evidence. The website's examples and interactive concept do not run physical trials.

Does it reset the world automatically?

Projected targets guide a person in restoring the scene. Camera checks can help verify the result. Automatic object manipulation or perfect reconstruction of every scene is not a promise of this product page.

Does it decide whether a task succeeded?

Scoring requires an explicit rubric and review process. The broader platform direction includes model-assisted review, but scene verification and outcome scoring answer different questions. A correctly placed cup can still be grasped unsuccessfully.
