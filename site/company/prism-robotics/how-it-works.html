Before the robot moves,
make the scene legible.

An evaluation begins before the first action and ends after the final score. Prism's job is to keep the physical setup connected to everything that follows.

First, decide what the trial means.

Name the task in terms someone else can recognize. “Put the mug in the tray” is a start. Does the handle matter? Must the mug remain upright? Is contact with another object a failure? What happens when a camera loses the view?

Write the success rule, the timeout, the allowed intervention and the reasons a trial may be invalid. Freeze them before comparing policies. A better reset does not resolve an ambiguous rubric.

Give light and vision a common frame.

The projector and camera see the same workspace from different places. Registration connects their views so an instruction lands where it belongs. Mounting, focus, exposure, surface geometry and the usable field of view all affect the result.

Check that relationship against the actual table. Keep a calibration reference with the setup. If the camera, projector or table moves, re-check the registration before treating an old scene as reproducible. Acceptance tolerances belong to the task; this site does not claim a universal accuracy figure.

Put the instruction where the hands are.

Choose the target condition. The projector marks placement guides on the work surface, and the operator brings the physical objects into the scene. The instruction is shared: the person preparing the table and the person reviewing the trial can refer to the same intended arrangement.

For a planar rigid object, an outline can communicate location and some orientation. It cannot fully specify the folds of a towel, the friction of a surface or the contents of an opaque container. Those conditions need additional controls.

Check the scene before asking the policy a question.

Compare the camera observation with the target condition. The useful outcome is not a decorative green light; it is an understandable decision about whether this setup is acceptable for this trial.

Keep the intended condition, observed placement and unresolved mismatch distinct. When the scene cannot be verified, preserve that uncertainty and resolve it before including the episode in a comparison. Detection confidence alone is not a physical guarantee.

Let the trial happen under its declared conditions.

Once the operator accepts the setup, the robot runs through its own execution system. Guidance must be handled consistently: projected marks visible to the policy may become an unintended cue. Decide when they disappear, and verify that behavior in the recording.

Record the policy or checkpoint identity, robot configuration, relevant sensor settings, condition identifier and any intervention. Preserve a failed trial as carefully as a successful one. A failure is often the most useful thing the experiment produces.

Keep the reason beside the result.

Review the outcome against the declared rubric. Link the observation or recording that supports the decision, and separate setup-invalid episodes from policy failures. If a reviewer changes a score, keep the reason for the change.

This is the intended evidence chain for a useful evaluation system. Specific recording formats, scoring integrations and review workflows must be agreed for a deployment; the public website is not an API specification.

Make the interesting scene possible to find again.

When a new checkpoint arrives, return to a saved condition and stage it again. Re-check the physical state; do not assume yesterday's calibration and today's table are identical. Compare the new episode with the earlier one and keep the context of both.

The long-term product direction is a shared history of scenes and results. Explore the interactive scene-history concept or use the evaluation planning guide to prepare a first experiment.
